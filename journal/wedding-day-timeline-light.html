How to build a wedding timeline around light

There is one hour on your wedding day when photographs come out markedly better than at any other time. Almost every schedule we are sent has something else booked in it.

2 July 2026 7 minute read Kshanam Studio

Photographers are tedious about light for a boring reason: it is the one variable we cannot buy our way out of. We can work in bad light — that is the job, and a competent photographer will get you usable photographs under fluorescent tubes at midnight. But there is a difference between photographs that survived the conditions and photographs that were helped by them, and the difference is almost entirely scheduling.

The good news is that it costs nothing. Moving one item in a running order by forty minutes is free. Here is how to think about it.

The hour that matters

Roughly the last hour before sunset, the sun is low enough that its light passes through more atmosphere, scatters, and arrives soft, warm and directional. Skin looks good in it. Nobody squints. Shadows have shape instead of being black holes. It is the single most flattering light that falls on your wedding for free.

Then, for about twenty minutes after the sun has actually gone, there is a second window that most people do not know about: the sky itself becomes the light source, enormous and even, and portraits taken in it have a particular quiet quality that is very hard to fake. We will often ask for ten minutes in this window specifically.

So: find out what time the sun sets on your date, at your venue. Write it at the top of the running order. Then build backwards.

The one request we make

If we ask you for a single change to a wedding schedule, it is this: put your couple portraits in the forty minutes before sunset. Not at two in the afternoon, which is when they usually get scheduled because it is convenient. That one move does more for your photographs than any equipment either of us owns.

What tends to go wrong

Portraits scheduled at midday. Overhead sun is the hardest light of the day: it puts shadows under eyes, blows out foreheads, and makes everyone squint. It is survivable — open shade, a verandah, a doorway — but it is the worst hour you could have chosen and it is chosen constantly because it sits neatly between the ceremony and lunch.

Everything indoors, late. If the ceremony is at seven in the evening and the reception follows in the same hall, there is no daylight anywhere in the day and every photograph will be lit by us. That is a legitimate wedding and we will light it properly, but it is worth knowing in advance that the look will be different, and it is worth stealing fifteen minutes outside before the light goes entirely.

The gap nobody planned. Group portraits take much longer than anyone expects: about three minutes per configuration once you include finding the people. Twelve groups is thirty-six minutes, not ten. If that overruns into the sunset window, the couple portraits get cut to six rushed minutes.

Getting ready in a dark room. Preparations are often photographed in a hotel room with the curtains shut and the ceiling lights on, which is the least attractive lighting in any building. Open the curtains. Turn the overhead lights off. Sit near the window. This single change improves the first hour of coverage more than anything else, and it costs nothing.

A shape that works

For a daytime ceremony with an evening reception, something close to this tends to work well. Adjust the clock to your own sunset.

  • Preparations — two hours before the ceremony, in the brightest room available, curtains open, artificial lights off.
  • Ceremony — wherever it must be. This one is not negotiable and we work with what the room gives us.
  • Immediately after — twenty minutes of nothing scheduled. This is when the best unposed photographs of the entire day happen, and a schedule that herds everyone straight into lunch loses all of them.
  • Group portraits — finishing at least forty-five minutes before sunset. Give us a list beforehand and nominate a loud relative who knows everyone by sight; they will save you fifteen minutes.
  • Couple portraits — the last forty minutes of daylight, plus ten minutes after the sun has gone.
  • Reception — after dark, which is the right time for it. Speeches and dancing photograph well in artificial light in a way that portraits do not.

Things worth asking the venue

Three questions that change our plan for the day:

  • Which direction do the main windows face? East-facing rooms are beautiful in the morning and flat by afternoon. West-facing rooms are the reverse.
  • Is there open shade outdoors? A covered verandah, a tree, the north side of a building. These are the places midday portraits actually work.
  • Can the house lights be dimmed during the first dance? If the answer is no, the first dance will be lit by whatever the ceiling provides, and we will plan accordingly.

Monsoon changes the maths

In Kerala, between June and September, the thing that limits you is not the position of the sun but the cloud in front of it. This is less of a disaster than it sounds: heavy overcast is an enormous soft light source, and it is genuinely forgiving for portraits at any hour of the day. What it removes is the sunset window, because there is no visible sunset.

So monsoon scheduling flips: the hour before sunset stops being special, and the thing to protect instead is a covered location that photographs well, and enough slack in the running order to move outside during a twenty-minute break in the rain. We have taken some of our favourite photographs in exactly those twenty minutes.

None of this requires you to design your wedding around a camera. It is one conversation, usually half an hour, and most of it is us asking what time things happen and where the windows are. But it is the difference between a photographer working against your day and working with it — and the schedule is the only part of that which has to be decided in advance.
