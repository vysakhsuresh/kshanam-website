Twelve questions to ask a wedding photographer

Most couples ask about price and portfolio. Neither one tells you what will happen on the day. These are the questions that do — and we have included the answers that should worry you.

18 August 2026 8 minute read Kshanam Studio

A portfolio is a highlight reel. Every photographer with a website has twenty good photographs, and twenty good photographs can come from two hundred weddings or from four. A price list is worse: it tells you what a studio costs, not what it does, and the cheapest quote and the most expensive quote on your shortlist are frequently offering completely different things under the same word.

What actually determines whether you like your wedding photographs is a set of unglamorous operational facts — who turns up, how long they stay, what happens to the files, what happens if something goes wrong. Those facts are easy to establish and almost nobody asks about them.

Here are the twelve questions we would ask, in the order we would ask them. We are a working studio, so we get asked these; we have noted what a good answer sounds like and what should end the conversation.

1. Who, specifically, will photograph our wedding?

In larger studios the person who shows you the portfolio is frequently not the person who will be at your wedding. That is not automatically a problem — plenty of big studios have excellent teams — but you want it said out loud, and you want to see that specific photographer’s work, not the studio’s best-of.

A bad answer: “One of our photographers.” Press until you have a name and a portfolio belonging to that name.

2. How many hours is that, and what happens when they are up?

“Full day coverage” means different things to different studios: eight hours, ten, twelve, or until the reception ends. Get the number. Then ask what the overtime rate is, because the single most common wedding-day friction is a photographer packing up at the moment things get interesting.

Ask it the other way round too: if the schedule collapses and the ceremony runs ninety minutes late, does the coverage window move, or does it end at the time printed on the contract?

3. How many finished photographs do we get, and what does finished mean?

Counts are nearly meaningless without this second half. Six hundred photographs where four hundred are near-duplicates and nothing has been colour corrected is a smaller delivery than three hundred finished frames. Ask whether every delivered image is individually corrected or whether a preset is applied to the batch. Both are legitimate; they are not the same product.

4. When do we get them?

Three weeks is good. Six is normal. Twelve is common and should be stated upfront rather than discovered in April. The warning sign is not a long timeline, it is a vague one — “as soon as possible” is how a four-month wait begins.

Ask separately about films, which legitimately take longer than stills because sound editing is slow.

The question behind the question

Every item on this list is really asking the same thing: has this studio done enough weddings to have a policy, and are they willing to put the policy in writing? A photographer who has thought about what happens when it rains has thought about a lot of other things too.

5. Do we own the photographs?

Almost certainly not — the photographer keeps copyright, which is standard practice and reasonable. What matters is the licence you get. You want the right to print, share and reproduce the photographs for personal use, permanently, without per-print fees and without asking permission each time.

A bad answer: anything involving a charge to print your own wedding photographs, or a gallery that expires.

6. Can our family download the full-resolution files?

Ask this one specifically. Some galleries are view-only, some allow web-sized downloads, some charge relatives per image. Your parents are going to want prints, and the awkwardness of that conversation lands on you, not the studio.

7. What happens to the files on the night of the wedding?

The answer you want is dull: cards are copied to at least two separate drives before anyone sleeps, and the cards are not reformatted until the copies are verified. Card failure is rare and unrecoverable, and the only defence is procedure.

Ask how long they keep your files afterwards. Years is good. “Until we deliver” means that if you lose your copy in 2031, it is gone.

8. What happens if you are ill on the morning?

This is the question most studios hope you will skip, and the most revealing one on the list. A photographer who has thought about it has an arrangement: a network of photographers whose work they would vouch for, a written commitment to arrange a replacement at their own cost, and a refund of their fee if they cannot deliver.

A bad answer: “That has never happened.” It has never happened yet. The point of the question is whether there is a plan.

9. Are you insured?

Public liability insurance matters because a light stand in a crowded hall is a real risk, and an increasing number of venues now require proof of it before letting a supplier through the door. Equipment insurance matters to them, not to you, but a studio carrying neither is telling you something about how it is run.

10. What will you ask us to do on the day?

Here you are establishing style in operational terms rather than adjectives. Some photographers will want ninety minutes of group portraits and a staged first look. Some will want twenty minutes near sunset and nothing else. Some will ask for nothing at all.

None of those is wrong. But if you are imagining a day where nobody interrupts you and you have hired someone who works from a shot list of forty posed configurations, you have both got a problem, and it will surface at four in the afternoon rather than now.

11. Have you worked at our venue, or in conditions like it?

Less important than it sounds for an experienced photographer, but revealing in the answer. What you are listening for is whether they think in terms of light and space: a dark church at noon, a hall with green fluorescent tubes, an outdoor mandapam in full sun, a reception that starts after dark. A photographer who answers with how they would handle the light has answered well, whether or not they know the building.

12. Is there a reason we should not book you?

Ask it directly. The best answer you can get is an honest one — that they are wrong for formal group portraits, or that they do not do traditional ritual coverage well, or that your date is three weeks after another commitment in another state.

A studio that cannot name a single thing it is not good at either has not been working long or is not being straight with you. We would rather lose a booking on the first call than photograph a wedding that wanted something we do not do. It is a worse business strategy and a much better way to spend somebody’s wedding day.

What this list is not

It is not a test to catch anyone out. Most photographers will answer all twelve without hesitation, and the exercise mostly tells you who you want to spend fourteen hours with — which, in the end, is the actual decision you are making. You are not buying four hundred photographs. You are choosing a person to stand at the edge of the most crowded day of your life and watch it carefully.

Ask the twelve questions. Then book whoever you would be happy to see at breakfast.
