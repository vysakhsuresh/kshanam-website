Candid or traditional? What those words mean in a quote

Every studio in India now advertises candid photography, which has made the word almost completely useless. Here is how to work out what you are actually being sold.

21 May 2026 6 minute read Kshanam Studio

Fifteen years ago “candid” meant something specific in Indian wedding photography. It distinguished a photographer who followed the day from one who assembled it — rows of relatives facing forward, the couple garlanding each other twice because the first time was missed, a flash on the camera for all of it.

Then candid worked as marketing, and now everybody uses it, including studios whose actual practice has not changed at all. So the word has stopped doing its job, and you need a different way to find out what will happen at your wedding.

What the three words tend to mean

Traditional

The photographer organises the day for the camera. Rituals are photographed from the front, at the moment they are most legible; groups are arranged in rows; the couple is directed throughout. The deliverable is a complete, formal, clearly identifiable record: every relative present and recognisable, every ritual documented in order.

This gets dismissed online and it should not be. For a lot of families it is exactly correct — if the album's job is to show your grandmother's generation that every ceremony was performed properly and every guest was honoured, a documentary photographer wandering around looking for interesting light is going to fail you badly.

Candid, as originally meant

The photographer does not intervene. They learn the running order, position themselves, and wait. Nothing is re-staged, groups are minimal, and the couple is mostly left alone. The deliverable is a record of how the day felt rather than a catalogue of what occurred.

The trade-off is real and worth stating: you will get fewer photographs in which everybody is looking at the camera and smiling, and you may not get a frame of every single relative. Some families mind this a great deal.

Candid, as currently advertised

Frequently it means: a traditional approach, plus a second photographer whose job is to catch reaction shots, plus heavier colour grading. Sometimes it means the posed portraits are now done outdoors at sunset instead of indoors with flash. Occasionally it means nothing whatsoever beyond the word appearing on the website.

None of that is fraud. But it is why the label cannot be trusted, and why the only reliable method is to ask about behaviour rather than about style.

One question that settles it

“Walk me through what you will ask us to do between the ceremony ending and dinner starting.”

A traditional photographer will describe a sequence of group configurations. A documentary photographer will describe twenty minutes of portraits near sunset and otherwise leaving you alone. Both answers are good answers. The one you want depends entirely on which of those afternoons you would rather have.

How to work out which you want

Forget the vocabulary and answer these instead.

  • Who is the album for? If the honest answer includes a wider family who will judge it on completeness, you need substantial traditional coverage whatever else you book.
  • How do you feel about being photographed? People who dislike it are usually far happier with a documentary approach, because the discomfort mostly comes from being directed. People who enjoy being photographed often feel neglected by it.
  • Is there a ritual that must be recorded properly? Name it explicitly in the brief. A documentary photographer will happily take the one formal frame that matters, but only if they know it matters.
  • Which do you regret more: a photograph nobody is in, or a photograph nobody meant? This is the whole decision, compressed.

You are allowed to want both

Most weddings we photograph are a mix, and the mix is agreed in advance rather than improvised. A typical shape: documentary coverage for the whole day, a defined list of eight to twelve group portraits, a short set of couple portraits in the last of the daylight, and formal frames of two or three specific rituals that the family has asked for by name.

That arrangement works because the expectations are written down. The weddings that go wrong are the ones where the couple imagined being left alone and the photographer arrived with a forty-item shot list, or the reverse — a family expecting a complete formal record and a photographer who spent the afternoon shooting the backs of people's heads in interesting light.

Two warning signs

A portfolio with no quiet frames at all. If every photograph is a hero shot of the couple and there is nothing of guests, hands, parents or rooms, the studio is selling you a fashion shoot that happens to occur at a wedding. That is a real product and some people want it. Just know that is what it is.

A portfolio where every frame looks like a different photographer took it. Wildly inconsistent colour across a single gallery usually means the editing is outsourced per-wedding, which also tells you something about how long delivery will take.

For what it is worth, we work the documentary way, with a short agreed set of portraits and whatever formal ritual frames a family asks for. We say so plainly because the alternative — claiming to be all things — is how couples end up with photographs of a wedding they did not have.

If what you want is a full formal record, there are studios in Kerala who do it beautifully and we will happily name a few.
